THE STUDY

Abstract

Human skin contains diverse mechanoreceptors, including Merkel cells and Pacinian corpuscles, each localized in specific regions. Although their spatial organization is known to be shaped by molecular signaling and neural induction during development, experimentally examining its functional significance under controlled conditions in living systems remains challenging. In particular, physically comparing alternative receptor arrangements that do not occur biologically is difficult.

In this study, we developed a layered biomimetic robotic skin designed to mimic key structural features of human skin tissue by embedding receptor-analog sensors within compliant soft media. Using this platform, we implemented both biologically motivated and counterfactual sensory arrangements and quantitatively evaluated their performance across three passive tactile tasks: detection of weak contacts, a two-point discrimination-like assessment, and texture identification. Arrangements reflecting key features of the biologically motivated sensory organization exhibited higher performance than the alternative configurations examined. This study addresses a biological question concerning how spatial organization influences tactile function by presenting a biomimetic robotic approach that enables physical and empirical comparison of biologically motivated and counterfactual sensory arrangements that are difficult to investigate in living systems.

THE QUESTION

Why does receptor placement matter?

Human skin contains mechanoreceptors with different response properties, distributed at different depths. Their organization develops through molecular signaling and neural induction, making it difficult to independently rearrange receptors in a living system and compare the consequences.

This work uses a robot to make that comparison physically possible. Receptor-like sensors are embedded in a soft, layered skin, allowing both biologically motivated arrangements and alternative configurations to be constructed and tested under controlled conditions.

How does changing the spatial organization of sensors change what the skin can feel?

THE PLATFORM

A tissue-level model of skin

The artificial skin combines a thin silicone-rubber epidermis, a compliant silicone-gel dermis, and a softer silicone-foam subcutaneous layer. Strain gauges act as pressure-sensitive analogs of Merkel cells; accelerometers act as vibration-sensitive analogs of Pacinian corpuscles. The construction preserves selected structural principles of biological skin.

THE EXPERIMENTS

Three ways to probe tactile function

  1. 01

    Weak-contact detection

    Detect small contact events from strain-gauge signals using a threshold based on background responses.

  2. 02

    Two-point discrimination

    Use HIRO-NX to apply single-point and two-point stimuli, comparing classification accuracy as their spacing changes from 5 to 15 mm.

  3. 03

    Texture identification

    Use PR2 to stroke metal, cloth, cork, and roughened cork over the skin at 2, 3, and 6 cm/s. Classify textures using features extracted from the vibration signals.

THE FINDINGS

Arrangement changes tactile performance

Superficial pressure sensors detect weaker contacts

Pattern B detected 17 of 20 weak-contact events, compared with 3 for Pattern C and none for Pattern A. These measurements support the benefit of placing pressure-sensitive elements near the surface for this task.

Depth and density affect spatial discrimination

Classification accuracy generally improved as the distance between contact points increased. Pattern B showed a pronounced improvement at 10 mm. Using all eight sensors in Patterns B and C improved performance relative to using their four shallower sensors alone. Performance at wider spacing also depended on the receptive field and the finite size of the sensor region.

Texture classification depends on the stroking condition

All arrangements supported texture identification. Pattern B achieved 95%, 95%, and 90% classification accuracy at 2, 3, and 6 cm/s, respectively. The table also shows that Pattern C reached 100% at 6 cm/s: no arrangement was uniquely best at every speed.

A balance across tactile tasks

The study combines task performance in a Tactile Discrimination Performance Index (TDPI), varying the weights of texture classification, light-touch detection, and two-point discrimination. Pattern B performs well over a broad range of weights. When comparing only A and C, the preferred arrangement changes with the tactile function emphasized.

INTERPRETATION

A physical approach to biological questions

The results suggest that biologically motivated sensory organization can provide advantages across the passive tactile tasks examined here. More broadly, the platform makes alternative body organizations experimentally accessible and connects biological questions with reproducible robotic experiments.

The scope is the specific configurations tested. The skin reproduces selected tissue-level features, while sensor dimensions and mechanical constraints limit density and placement. The experiments therefore provide evidence within this design space; they do not establish a universally optimal arrangement or a direct explanation of evolutionary selection.
